I am a Wierd Girl

Moe Frank

I am a weird girl. Sometimes I don’t feel like a girl at all. Yelena says I must be an alien, or some wild thing–Yelena is my classmate, brown frizzy hair and stripey blue and white pigtail holders. Yelena says I look sooo pretty and then laughs, a funny cackling laugh like a wild African dog. I read about them in a book from the public library and decided I loved them, that they were my new favorite animal. When I told Yelena that she made a face that was about 70% frown and 30% something I couldn’t name, and told me see, this is why no one in our grade likes you.

When she says things like that my heart twists in my chest and I feel bad, bad, bad. I like groupings of three; they make me fizzy and green. I like reading, too. Judy Blume and Molly Moon and Madeline L’Engle. I was so proud that I learned how to pronounce L’Engle. I practiced over and over and over on the floor of my room until it rolled off my tongue like dewdrops on a leaf, and I couldn’t help myself from smiling with all my teeth, the smile that makes Granny say I look crazy.

I walked into class today and there was a new girl sitting in the very back corner. She had tall stompy boots on, black with electric blue eyelets, and her hair was chin-length and dark with one stripey red streak. I think I stared too long, because her big brown eyes turned narrow and crinkled when she looked at me. Her mouth turned down and creased at the corners. I looked away fast.

The teacher told her to introduce herself. I guess she didn’t want to, because she stomped on up to the front of the classroom and sighed once, loud and annoyed, before she spoke.

“My name’s Andy,” she said, and her eyes rolled, rolled, rolled like two beer-bottle marbles. “I moved here from Pasadena.” And that was all.

My eyes darted to her, again and again and again, as algebra dragged on. After class I gathered my bravery and walked on up to her desk.

“I’m sorry for staring,” I said. I made my voice quiet and a little blurry, because it was better than the alternative - loud and annoying. “I wanted to tell you that your hair looks like a red-winged blackbird. I like it.”

Andy frowned, but it wasn’t a frown like Yelena’s. I couldn’t tell if that was a good thing or not, but her eyebrows weren’t as wrinkled, and the creases at the corners of her eyes were gone this time. She said, “Thanks.”

I nodded once, and then couldn’t stop myself from nodding two more times. She studied me for a moment, and I was sure she hated me, that I’d messed it up like I did everything. But then she opened her mouth and said:

“Come sledding with me.”

It was chilly outside and the snow swam down in wet scraps. There was barely enough of it for your feet to crunch on. I said yes anyways.

We perched atop Sugarloaf Hill, and my heart pounded and pounded and pounded. The grass was frosty and felt slick like soap bubbles under my boot. We hadn’t pushed off yet, and so my dumb old mouth thought it an appropriate time to ask: “Do you think I’m weird?”

The wind made her voice faint, but I still heard her when she said, “Weird? Yeah. Like me, you’re weird. But that makes us cool. Better than them.”

My mouth worked against my bottom lip for a moment. And then I said, “Your favorite word. Um. What is it? Mine is poignant.” I said it carefully, and hoped against hope she’d be careful in turn. I had given her my heart and it was wet and fragile in her hands.

She turned around to face me, like it was important that I see her. She smiled and I saw nearly all her teeth. Her left canine was sunshine yellow. “Conflagration,” she said, and pushed us off. The fall came slowly, and then all at once. My breath caught in my chest.

The cold air bit at my face, but it was a love bite, and her hair flapped in the wind, the red streak like a victory banner. We flew, we flew, we flew down the hill, two wild birds.

Jack Poem

Juno Farschi

All You Good People

when jack died i didn’t tell anyone./not my mother, or my father,/not even my boyfriend/they didn’t need to know that jack was dead,/because jack wasn’t dead./there was no shot, no rhyme or reason why jack would be dead./he was just dead.

a girl who i used to be a girl with,/who wasn’t quite a person any longer,/but a poltergeist on my bedpost,/named tallyn,/like the hand of a killing bird./like the shoulder of a lock bolt,/told me over the phone/that jack was dead./and i said oh no,/and okay/and unpaused my episode of Girls/and didn’t tell anybody.

his mom strung up christmas lights, took over his instagram account./she’d post the bulbs flickering, dying in and out/she’d say he’s here! my son! my son is home!/she’d send messages from his phone, just to light up the hearts of his grieving contact list./she’d post pictures of jesus,/of saints, of sinners,/and say my baby! there’s my baby!

for her peace/he became the kind of person/who is not even a person anymore./he was the holy triplet,/he was too, the traitor.

but never did jack, as she hoped he would, come home and hold her tight and say/I forgive you, Mama/never could jack live again, as she pictured him being/when she fell asleep/alone and drunk/with his phone on her charger

jack was dead in the bedroom for some hours before anyone noticed, a towel stuffed under the door and the windows bared wide open.

they held a funeral at a church, but didn’t invite anybody. so tallyn and the others lit up in her bedroom.

this is what he would’ve wanted./he would’ve wanted to hear this./he would’ve wanted to hear from me again,/i think.

in the church, his mom was inconsolable. an idiot/who wasn’t really an idiot at all,/weeping about god,/in front of good people./good people who/ignored jack,/in his time of need,/who killed him, really,/who called jack a f-----.

these are the people,/i kept thinking,/these are the good people who called jack a f-----./who’d come for tea/with his mom/while he tried oxy/in a grown man’s bedroom.

these are the shiny people who said these things./these are the shiny people pretending to mourn./all of these shiny people,/waiting for someone to come back from the dead.

these are the shiny people who never really met jack/the way i met jack.

i used to hide with jack in bathrooms, in ditches, in the spaces between bedrooms where people filmed videos of other people

i used to know jack/the way jack was

in that house/in my house/in our school/where we were children

i knew jack

his mom says he’ll come back as a light.

but i knew jack

jack isn’t a light/jack isn’t shiny

you’re shiny,

you live in rooms without people./rooms where people aren’t really people/you live in spaces between curtains,/pretending to be curtains./pretending to be people.

you’re shiny,

and jack is dead
